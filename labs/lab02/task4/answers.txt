Task 4 observations
====================

(a) Delay = 1
----------------
and_df:         Correct
and_beh_before: Correct
and_beh_intra:  Correct

The inputs change every 2 time units, so no input change occurs while either
behavioral process is waiting for its 1-unit delay.

(b) Delay = 2
----------------
and_df:         Correct delayed-dataflow behavior
and_beh_before: Incorrect for the intended delayed AND behavior
and_beh_intra:  Incorrect for the intended delayed AND behavior

The input changes occur exactly as frequently as the delay. The continuous
assignment keeps responding to every input change. The behavioral processes,
however, are blocked by their procedural delays and therefore can miss input
changes.

For and_beh_before, the inputs are evaluated AFTER the delay, so it may use
the newer values that exist when the delay expires.

For and_beh_intra, the AND expression is evaluated BEFORE the delay, so the
value being written is captured from an earlier input state. Input changes
that occur while the process is waiting are not separately handled.

(c) Delay = 3
----------------
and_df:         Correct delayed-dataflow behavior
and_beh_before: Incorrect
and_beh_intra:  Incorrect

Now the inputs change several times while the behavioral process is blocked.
The before-assignment version evaluates whatever the inputs are when the
delay finishes. The intra-assignment version has already captured an older
value. Neither behaves like a continuously delayed version of the AND gate.

The dataflow assignment continues to react to input changes and models the
delayed combinational connection directly.

(d) Overall observations
------------------------
For a delay of 1, all three implementations appear equivalent because the
inputs do not change faster than the behavioral process can react.

As the delay becomes equal to or greater than the input-change interval,
the behavioral versions stop being equivalent to the dataflow version.

The important distinction is:

    #D y = a & b;

waits first and evaluates a & b later.

    y = #D a & b;

evaluates a & b immediately and delays only the assignment.

    assign #D y = a & b;

is a continuous dataflow assignment with its own delay semantics and keeps
responding to changes in its inputs.

(e) What this teaches about simulation delays
---------------------------------------------
A simulation delay is not simply "make this operation take D time."

Where the delay is placed determines when the input expression is evaluated
and what happens when the inputs change while the process is delayed.

For combinational logic with propagation delay, a delayed continuous
assignment is often the most direct model:

    assign #D y = expression;

A procedural delay can introduce behavior that depends on the simulator's
event scheduling and on whether the procedural process is blocked while
inputs continue changing.

Therefore, when testing delayed logic, the stimulus should change at times
that are comparable to or faster than the modeled delay. Otherwise, different
delay placements can appear identical even though they have fundamentally
different simulation behavior.
